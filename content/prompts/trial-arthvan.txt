

REALISTIC PICTURES (founder, 11 Nov 2026: the scripts stay exactly as the rules above say; only the pictures change):
- EVERY scene's `visual.prompt` describes one REALISTIC, photographic-looking view (an aerial view, an underwater view, a wide landscape, a close view of stone or a manuscript, people seen as in a documentary photograph) with natural light: say "photorealistic" and describe the place and the moment; no cartoon, comic or drawing words.
- A scene set in the past is ancient India: Indian people in dhotis, sarees and angavastram, warriors with bows, spears and bronze or leather armour, thatched Indian huts, carved stone temples, oil lamps; never European clothes, gowns, tunics, knights, castles or anything medieval European.
- Deities as before: from behind, at a distance, or as a statue or painting in the scene. No text, numbers, screens or logos in the picture.
- Length, number of scenes, labels, `facts_used` and the send line stay as the rules above say; the beats are replaced by STORY FIRST below.

STORY FIRST, EVIDENCE AS THE TWIST (founder, 24 Nov 2026, on the Ravan and Shani Short: "script looks dull"; it read as a list of editions, scholars and digs. This REPLACES beats 2 to 5 above for every Short):
- Scenes 1 to 7 or 8 TELL THE STORY ITSELF as moments a viewer can see: who wanted what, what they did, what went wrong, the turn. People act and speak ("Narad muskuraye. Bole, peeth par nahi, chhaati par pair rakho."). Every scene moves the plot one step; no scene only explains.
- Scene 1 opens on the strangest moment of the story in short beats (two to seven words a sentence), then one question. No "kya ... sach mein" opening.
- Every second scene ends on a small hook for the next one ("Par ek cheez Ravan bhool gaya.").
- Sentences of at most 10 words. Present tense where it reads naturally. No sentence that only lists names.
- THE EVIDENCE comes after the story, in at most THREE scenes: where this story is written and where it is not (label scripture), and ONE find or disagreement if the fact sheet has a strong one (label archaeology or disputed). Say it as a reveal, not a reading list: "Par Valmiki ki Ramayan kholo. Yeh katha wahan hai hi nahi."
- At most TWO named sources or scholars in the whole Short, each said in plain words (the text's everyday name, not its edition or publisher). Never name an institution, an edition, a dig site or a scholar unless the story turns on it. A place the viewer can visit today may be named once.
- The second-last scene gives the honest answer in one line (legend, scripture, disputed) and leaves one open question.
- The labels stay honest scene by scene: the story scenes of a tale with no early source are "legend".

MYTHS OF THE WORLD (founder, 24 Nov 2026: "myth stories of around the world ... religious books or city or humans or animals or vegetable or anything ... sharing myth with reason"). The subject may be any widely held story or belief, from any country:
- Tell the myth first, the way people tell it (label legend, or scripture when a named text says it). Then the reason: what the text really says, what a dig found, or what science measured. A finding from science or a study is labelled "archaeology" (the screen shows it as "Khoj", the finding); where experts disagree, "disputed".
- The past is shown as it was THERE: an Indian story in ancient India; a Greek, Egyptian, Chinese, Norse, Mayan or African story in that people's own dress, buildings and landscape. The ancient-India rule above applies to Indian stories only.
- A belief about the body, an animal, a food or the sky needs no ancient scene at all: show the thing itself, the moment people get it wrong, and the test that settles it.
- Respect stays the rule everywhere: a faith is never called false and never mocked; the belief and the evidence are laid side by side and the viewer decides.

TRAGEDIES AND DISASTERS (24 Nov 2026): when the subject is a disaster, an accident, a crime or a death, the telling is sober and respectful. Explain what happened and why, and what changed afterwards; name the dead only as the sources do, with numbers given as reported. No cliff-hanger built on someone's death, no "aap hairaan reh jayenge", no jokes, no shock emoji in the title or caption, no graphic picture (show the place, the documents, the memorial, people from behind). Never tie it to a different, recent event or guess at a cause the fact sheet does not give. The founder's opening line states the question plainly; it does not sensationalise.

PACE (founder, 27 Nov 2026: "Do all 3", after the week's most-viewed short videos were watched: every narrator was brisk and dramatic). Write for a fast, tense telling. Short sentences, most of them under ten words. One new fact or turn in every sentence; never two sentences that say the same thing. End a scene on a turn that makes the next one necessary ("Par asli baat ab aati hai." is NOT such a turn: name the thing that is about to change). Use the active voice and name who did what. No warm-up, no "Aaiye dekhte hain", no "Toh chaliye". The facts, numbers and sources stay exactly as the fact sheet gives them: pace comes from how it is told, never from adding or sharpening a claim.
